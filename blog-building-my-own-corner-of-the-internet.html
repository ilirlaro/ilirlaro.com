Building my own corner of the internet

Why I wanted somewhere that could accumulate as my own body of work rather than disappearing into the companies, platforms and feeds I happen to use.

Ilir Laro

Personal observations shaped by work across crypto, product, growth and financial technology.

Most of my work has lived somewhere else

For most of my career, the things I have created have belonged to the organisations I was working for. That is how employment is supposed to work, and I am proud of many of those projects, but it creates an odd effect over time. You can spend years building strategies, launches, processes and ideas, then move company and realise that very little of that work exists anywhere that is actually yours.

The same thing happens online more broadly. A lot of what we write or share now lives inside platforms designed around feeds. Something can take hours to think through and then effectively disappear after a day. Your online identity becomes a sequence of posts, job updates and whatever an algorithm happens to surface rather than a body of work that develops deliberately over time.

I wanted somewhere that could accumulate

This website started because I wanted the opposite of that. I wanted one place that could become more useful and more representative of me as I added to it. The case studies document work I am proud of, the About page captures parts of my life that do not fit into a CV, and this Ideas section gives me somewhere to work through thoughts without needing them to become company content or social-media posts.

I also wanted the friction of having to decide what deserves to stay. A personal site has limited space in a way that an infinite feed does not. If something is going to sit here for years, I need to be more comfortable with it representing how I think. That does not mean every piece needs to be perfect or permanently correct. It means I want to write with enough care that changing my mind later feels like progress rather than embarrassment.

I wanted somewhere on the internet that became more mine the more I used it.

Building it is part of the point

I have had ideas for personal projects for years and have often been better at thinking about them than actually putting them into the world. It is easy to keep improving the imaginary version because it never has to encounter real constraints. A website is a small way of breaking that habit. Every page forces a decision about what I want to say, how I want it to look and whether I am willing to publish it before I have solved everything.

That has been unexpectedly useful. Building something for yourself exposes preferences that are easy to ignore when you are working inside someone else's brand. I have had to decide how formal I want to sound, what parts of my career matter enough to document, what I am comfortable making personal and what I would rather leave private. Those decisions are part design, part writing and part figuring out what I want this next stage of my life to contain.

A personal brand is not really the objective

I understand why people describe projects like this as personal branding, but that framing does not particularly motivate me. I am not trying to turn every interest into a content strategy or optimise my personality for an audience. If anything, I want a place that gives me permission to be broader than the professional version of myself.

My career is one part of that, but I am also interested in film, music, philosophy, physics, markets, entrepreneurship and questions about how people think. Some of those subjects may eventually connect to work and some probably never will. I would rather let the site become a record of those interests than force everything into one perfectly coherent professional niche.

Ownership changes how I think about time

The part I like most is that there is no real end state. A social profile tends to emphasise what is happening now, while a personal site can hold different versions of you at once. An essay from this year can sit beside something I write five years from now, and the difference between them may eventually be more interesting than either piece on its own.

That makes the site feel less like a launch and more like an archive I have only just started. I want to add work when there is something worth documenting, write when I have an idea I cannot stop thinking about and build other projects around it when they become real enough to share. If I do that consistently, the value will come from accumulation rather than from any individual page.

For now, that is enough

I do not know exactly what this becomes. It may stay primarily professional, grow into a larger collection of writing or eventually connect to businesses and creative projects I have not built yet. I am deliberately leaving that open because trying to define the finished version would probably recreate the same problem that stopped me starting in the first place.

For now, I have somewhere that is mine, a reason to publish rather than endlessly refine, and a place where the different parts of what I am working on can gradually meet. That feels like a useful enough beginning.
