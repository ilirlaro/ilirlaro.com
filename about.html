The foundations came before the career

My family came to London when I was one. I was born in Albania, but London is where I grew up and where most of my formative experiences took place. My parents had not been to university and did not come from corporate or business backgrounds, so there was no obvious blueprint for the kind of career I eventually built. What they gave me instead was a practical set of values that has stayed consistent: work hard, stay grounded, be sensible with money, look after the people around you and build things gradually rather than expecting progress to arrive quickly.

I also learned relatively early that ability is not fixed. I did not grow up assuming I was the most naturally talented person in the room, but over time I discovered that deliberate learning, repetition and context could change what I was capable of. That became an important professional principle for me. I tend to be less interested in whether somebody already knows everything and more interested in how quickly they can understand a problem, improve their judgement and become useful to the people around them.

My career has followed emerging industries rather than a conventional path

My first professional world was gaming and esports. I had grown up around games and internet culture, and I was drawn to an industry that was still forming its commercial models, communities and professional structures. That experience made me comfortable working in categories where the playbook is incomplete and where teams have to learn while the market itself is changing.

Crypto had many of the same characteristics when I moved into it. I joined the Litecoin Foundation through community and education work before taking on broader marketing and partnership responsibilities. At Binance, that widened into international growth, campaigns, operations, regulatory implementation and cross-functional go-to-market work. At Uphold, my focus moved further into consumer strategy, retention and monetisation. At Crypto.com, I have worked across product marketing and GTM for a growing portfolio of financial products. The progression looks broad on paper, but the common thread has been translating complex products and business objectives into something customers, markets and internal teams can act on.

I tend to follow the problem rather than the job description

I never made a deliberate decision to become broad across Product, Marketing, Growth and Operations. It happened because the problems I was responsible for rarely fitted neatly inside one function. A launch might begin as a marketing question and quickly become a product, legal, compliance, localisation or operational-readiness problem. A retention issue might look like a lifecycle problem until the data shows that the real friction sits in payments, pricing, product experience or customer service.

That has shaped how I work. I like understanding enough of the whole system to identify the constraint, bring the right people into the problem and create a practical route from strategy to execution. I am particularly comfortable in environments where the product is complex, the market is moving quickly, regulation matters and several teams have to align before anything meaningful can ship. My strongest work has usually been at those intersections rather than inside a narrow functional lane.

The work I enjoy most is taking something complicated, finding the underlying problem and turning it into a plan that multiple teams can actually execute.

What motivates me has become more practical over time

Earlier in my career, progression itself was a strong motivator. I wanted to learn quickly, take on larger responsibilities and prove to myself that I could operate at a higher level. I still care about ambition, but I increasingly judge work by whether it creates something useful: a clearer operating model, a stronger customer proposition, a better launch, a more capable team or a business decision that is grounded in evidence rather than assumption.

I also get a disproportionate amount of satisfaction from helping other people become more effective. That can mean giving somebody the context behind a decision, helping a junior team member structure a problem, or creating a process that removes ambiguity for several functions at once. I have worked in organisations ranging from lean teams to large global businesses, and one thing I have learned is that execution usually improves when people understand not only what they are being asked to do, but why it matters and how their work connects to the wider objective.

Curiosity is part of how I make decisions

Outside the immediate requirements of a role, I spend a lot of time thinking about markets, technology, incentives and how people make decisions under uncertainty. That is one reason financial products have held my attention for so long: they sit at the intersection of mathematics, psychology, regulation, narratives and human behaviour. A technically strong product can still fail because distribution is weak, trust is missing or the customer does not understand why the product matters.

I am also interested in philosophy and physics, particularly epistemology, perspective, space and time. Those subjects are personal interests, but they have a professional effect as well. They make me more conscious of assumptions, incomplete information and the difference between what a team knows, what it believes and what it can actually prove. I find that useful in strategy work, where confidence can easily become a substitute for evidence.

I want to spend more of my career building

Entrepreneurship and financial independence have interested me for years, but the motivation is increasingly about optionality rather than status. I want the ability to choose what I work on, build products or businesses I believe should exist, advise teams where my experience is genuinely useful and develop ideas outside the constraints of a single employer. The challenge for me has often been moving from analysis into action quickly enough, so I am deliberately trying to spend more time shipping, testing and learning in public.

This website is part of that shift. Most of my professional output has naturally belonged to the companies I worked for. I wanted a place where the accumulated thinking, case studies and lessons could become a body of work of my own. The Work section documents projects, the Blog gives me somewhere to develop ideas properly, and the Advisory work creates a practical way to apply what I have learned with founders and leadership teams.

Outside work, I try to keep a wider perspective

I am naturally fairly introverted and spend a lot of time with family, reading, watching films, listening to music or following subjects that have nothing to do with a current project. Film and music have always been important creative outlets for me, while family is the strongest reminder that professional milestones are only one part of a life. I still care deeply about building a successful career and creating financial security, but I also want enough independence to be present for the people around me and enough curiosity to keep exploring things that are not immediately useful.

That perspective affects how I work more than I expected when I was younger. Titles, organisations and individual projects change quickly, so I try not to confuse status with value. I have learned from people at every level of the companies I have worked in, and I tend to respect judgement, curiosity, reliability and the ability to make other people better far more than hierarchy. Those are the qualities I want to keep developing in my own work as the next stage of my career takes shape.
